An Introduction

What Does It Mean to Travel Through Time While Black?

Stuart Hall tracks how Black bodies get fixed inside a "racialized regime of representation" across centuries. bell hooks asks what it means to look back at that machinery, deliberately, with what she calls the oppositional gaze. Claudia Rankine, on Serena Williams, reads how excellence lives in public under a microscope of manners and race. Lily Kunda follows the media storm around Roc Nation and the NFL after Colin Kaepernick's kneeling made Black dissent into headline spectacle.

The Black spectacular as a site of shapeshifting: to be seen, refused, re-seen, re-refused, and then to build something else entirely from that refusal. Hooks traces this through cinema. Hall traces it through representation's deep history. Rankine and Kunda show how the same pressures travel through sports pages and partnership press releases. Together they map the archive these projects inherit and the cracks running through it.

The Red String

One continuous thread running through both texts

Hall and hooks are asking the same question from opposite ends of the same rope: what happens to the gaze when the gazed-upon decides to stare back?

01
The Body as Text

Hall identifies the racialized body as the primary surface onto which Western culture writes its anxieties. Through the history of slavery, colonial anthropology, and mass media, the Black body was made to function as "incontrovertible evidence" of natural difference. Difference that required no argument because it was visible. You could see it.

"The body itself and its differences were visible for all to see, and thus provided the incontrovertible evidence for a naturalization of racial difference."

This is the machinery that hooks's spectators are navigating when they walk into a theater. The body as text, already written before the film even begins. The screen becomes a second skin, and what is projected onto it confirms what was already decided elsewhere.

02
Fixing Meaning, Resisting Closure

Hall's argument about stereotyping is fundamentally temporal. The stereotype wants to arrest time, to declare that certain characteristics are fixed "forever in Nature." Naturalization is, as Hall frames it, a strategy against history itself, since if difference is natural it becomes immovable, beyond revision.

hooks captures the subjective experience of this freezing. Black women spectators who went to Hollywood films encountered a cinema that had pre-decided their meaning. Many cultivated an oppositional gaze: a way of looking that sidestepped the passive role the screen offered and refused the terms the film was selling.

"We see our history as counter-memory, using it as a way to know the present and invent the future."
03
The Spectacle and Its Double

Both texts circle around a structural ambivalence at the heart of racial representation. Hall's analysis of the Ben Johnson photograph makes this vivid: the same image can signify triumph and disgrace simultaneously. Black subjects are "required to be both things at the same time." Hero and villain. Noble and savage. Infantile and hypersexual.

This double-bind is exactly what the oppositional gaze exposes. hooks's Black female spectators developed a critical distance from the screen because the films rarely offered identification on terms that fit their lives. Being denied entry into the apparatus produced an alternative vantage point, a place from which to read the image against itself.

"Black and white audiences were reading the same thing differently all along."
04
Shapeshifting as Counter-Strategy

The end of Hall's chapter examines what he calls "trans-coding": taking existing meanings and re-appropriating them for different ends. This is representational shapeshifting. Hall stresses how hard it is to step wholly outside the regime of representation; trans-coding still works with the same vocabulary, bent toward new ends, bodies in motion inside the frame history built.

hooks arrives at the same site through a different door. Filmmakers like Julie Dash and Zeinabu Davis rework the apparatus itself: who holds the camera, what body sits at the center of the frame, whose gaze organizes the narrative. That is time travel in the Black spectacular sense, returning to the image-making machinery and rewiring it.

"As critical spectators, black women participate in a broad range of looking relations, contest, resist, revision, interrogate, and invent on multiple levels."
05
The Spectacular as Persistence

What connects Hall's historical archive to hooks's cinematic present is persistence. Hall documents how the representational figures forged in the era of slavery, the Tom, the Mammy, the Buck, the tragic mulatto, passed into American cinema almost intact. The genre kept shifting while the deep grammar held.

hooks saw this firsthand. The Black women spectators she interviewed were uniformly aware of cinematic racism. They expected nothing different. That expectation is itself a kind of temporal knowledge, a consciousness carried forward from before their own lifetimes, transmitted through culture. The spectacular operates across time. So does the opposition to it.

"Looking and looking back, black women involve ourselves in a process whereby we see our history as counter-memory."
06
Arena, Headline, Archive

Rankine's essay on Serena Williams keeps the body center stage the way Hall does in his readings of spectacle, only now the court is literal: slow-motion replays, dress-code debates, breathless profiles that praise and punish in the same paragraph. Williams compresses Hall's long history of visible "difference" into a single athlete's career arc.

"There is a belief among some African-Americans that to defeat racism, they have to work harder, be smarter, be better."

Kunda's study of the Roc Nation–NFL partnership picks up the same choreography in a corporate key. Press coverage framed Jay-Z as either savior or sellout while Kaepernick, largely silent in the deal's aftermath, stayed lodged in the public mind as the kneeling body around which the league staged its repair narrative. The question of who narrates Black protest beside white institutions lands close to hooks's account of who controls the look in classical Hollywood.

"Many accused the NFL of using the celebrity reputation of Roc-Nation founder, Jay-Z, for cultural capital to recoup from the negative press received following the 2016 protest of Colin Kaepernick."
Key Concepts

The terms that do the real work

bell hooks

The Oppositional Gaze

The gaze has always been political. Enslaved people were punished for looking at white people directly. The prohibition itself created a counter-desire, an "overwhelming longing to look" that hooks calls rebellious.

The oppositional gaze emerges from this history. It is a mode of looking that does not accept the terms of the image it encounters. Awareness. Refusal. Critical interrogation. All happening simultaneously while watching a film.

hooks, "The Oppositional Gaze," 1992
Stuart Hall

Regime of Representation

A regime of representation is the whole system of imagery through which a culture signals "difference" at a given historical moment. Hall traces this across centuries, from soap advertisements to Olympic photographs to Hollywood.

The regime is persistent while still shifting at the joints. Its stereotypes change in surface appearance while maintaining their deep grammar. Race, gender, and sexuality cross each other inside it in ways that multiply and reinforce the marking of difference.

Hall, "The Spectacle of the Other," 1997
Stuart Hall

Stereotyping as Splitting

Hall, drawing on Sander Gilman's psychoanalytic framework, argues that stereotyping always involves splitting the world into "good" and "bad" objects. The stereotype projects anxiety onto the Other, keeping the self clean.

This means stereotypes are structurally binary: the same Black subject can embody both the infantile and the hypersexual, the noble and the savage. The double-bind is built into the form itself, ahead of whatever any one viewer happens to believe.

Hall, "The Spectacle of the Other," 1997
bell hooks

Cinematic Gaslighting

hooks uses this term for the experience of Black women who loved Hollywood cinema. To fully enjoy the pleasure of the film, they had to "close down critique" and forget racism. The film asked them to participate in their own negation.

Toni Morrison's Miss Pauline Breedlove is hooks's example: a woman who finds happiness at the movies only by imagining herself as the white woman on screen. "But it made coming home hard."

hooks, "The Oppositional Gaze," 1992
Stuart Hall

Fetishism and Disavowal

In Hall's reading of Saartjie Baartman's public display in 19th century Europe, fetishism operates as a "having-it-both-ways": the observers can look obsessively while disavowing the sexual nature of their gaze by calling it science.

This is how forbidden desire finds representation. The fetish object substitutes for what cannot be directly shown. The alibi, Hall notes, is always power/knowledge, ethnology, objectivity, the pursuit of Truth.

Hall, "The Spectacle of the Other," 1997
bell hooks

Counter-Memory

hooks ends "The Oppositional Gaze" by connecting the look to history. Black women spectators who develop a critical gaze use it to see their own past as counter-memory: a way of knowing the present through what dominant narratives have suppressed.

This is where time travel enters the frame. Counter-memory refuses linear time. It assembles a different archive from the same materials, which is what the Black spectacular activates again and again.

hooks, "The Oppositional Gaze," 1992
Stuart Hall

Trans-Coding

Hall's term for taking an existing meaning and re-appropriating it for new ends. "Black is Beautiful" is the clearest example: it takes the very characteristic that had been coded as deficiency and re-inflects it as a source of value.

Trans-coding can never produce a final victory because meaning can never be permanently fixed. But the instability of meaning is also what makes intervention possible in the first place. The regime can be entered from within.

Hall, "The Spectacle of the Other," 1997
Stuart Hall + bell hooks

Commodity Racism

Hall, via Anne McClintock, tracks how Victorian advertising transformed the imperial encounter into domestic imagery. Soap. Biscuits. Tea. The colonial world enters the home as spectacle, cleaning the consumer's conscience alongside the laundry.

This is an early version of what hooks documents in cinema: the use of Black bodies to organize white fantasy, to produce white meaning, to maintain white comfort. Hall's Victorian examples arrive on the page; hooks's arrive in light, yet the architecture of the look rhymes.

Hall, "The Spectacle of the Other," 1997
Claudia Rankine

Black Excellence on Display

Rankine stays close to Serena Williams at the height of her dominance: the extra work of composure, the demand that triumph arrive with forgiveness ready at hand, the commentary that polices a Black woman's body for "too much" joy or swagger.

The essay makes Hall's "incontrovertible evidence" feel almost intimate. Millions of people think they already know what they are seeing when they watch her win. hooks's language about cinematic gaslighting helps here, too: the crowd wants the spectacle of Black excellence on its terms, on schedule, with the right facial expression.

Rankine, "The Meaning of Serena Williams," 2015
Lily Kunda

Celebrity, Protest, and the Broadcast Deal

Kunda reads the 2019 Roc Nation–NFL partnership as a media event where Jay-Z's cultural capital met Kaepernick's kneeling in the public imagination. News cycles asked, in effect, who speaks for Black dissent once institutions invite a famous face inside the boardroom.

The argument extends hooks's and Hall's questions about who authorizes images of Black life: here the arena is sports television, press releases, and Twitter threads, but the choreography of looking and being looked at rhymes with the cinema Hall and hooks dissect.

Kunda, "A knee at the table," 2025
Process Notes

How this project came together

  • Starting point The project began with a question about time travel as a structural practice. Nostalgia lingers nearby, yet the wager here is structural: how the Black spectacular becomes a site where past representations surface, get replayed, and get fought over in the present.
  • Source selection hooks and Hall anchor the site because they work the same terrain from different angles. hooks moves from lived experience outward; Hall maps systems of meaning inward. Rankine on Williams and Kunda on the NFL partnership extend the same questions into sports media where bodies, brands, and protest meet in real time.
  • The red string The organizing logic of the site came from a shared preoccupation across the readings: the problem of fixing and unfixing meaning. Each text asks what it takes to make meaning slide.
  • On the writing The goal was to stay academically grounded while treating each text as a living argument. Engagement means leaning on their claims, testing them against one another, and letting the contemporary readings push the archive forward.
  • What this leaves open The connection to contemporary Black performance, to the spectacular as it operates now in social media and streaming, is deliberately left as the next layer. This site maps the archivebut the project work builds from here.
